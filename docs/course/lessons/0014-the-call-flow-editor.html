The Call Flow editor

How an incoming number stops being a straight line to one desk and becomes a decision tree you draw on a canvas.

⏱ ~11 minutes🎯 Goal: read & reason about a call-flow graph🧩 Builds on: Lesson 11

Until now, an incoming number went somewhere simple: a desk, a team, a waiting room (Lesson 5). But real businesses want more: "during office hours, offer a menu — press 1 for sales, 2 for support; sales rings the sales queue; after hours, everything goes to voicemail." That is not one destination — it is a little map of decisions. The Call Flow editor is where you draw that map.

The one-liner. A Call Flow is a flowchart for a phone call. You drag boxes (a menu, a queue, a team, a voicemail) onto a canvas and connect them with wires. A number can point straight at the whole flow — because in Parrot, a Call Flow is itself a destination, just like a desk is.

The everyday picture

Think of the front desk of a big office with a flowchart taped to the wall: "Phone rings → is it open? → if yes, ask what they want → send them to the right team → if nobody picks up, take a message." The receptionist just follows the chart. A Call Flow is that chart, drawn in Odoo, and the switchboard is the receptionist who follows it — perfectly, every time.

It's one graph, stored as JSON

The whole diagram — every box, every wire, and where each box sits on the canvas — lives in a single JSON field, graph_data, on the voip.call.flow record. There is no table of "nodes" and "edges"; the canvas is the data.(voip_call_flow.py)

The important structural fact: voip.call.flow inherits voip.pbx.destination.mixin. That mixin is what makes something "a place a call can be sent." A desk (voip.extension) has it; a queue has it; and so does a whole Call Flow. So a DID number can set its destination to a Call Flow directly — the number rings, and the flowchart takes over.

Why this matters. "A Call Flow is a destination" is the hinge of the whole feature. It means everything you already learned about pointing a number at a destination (Lesson 5) still applies — the destination is just now a graph instead of a single desk.

The nodes — and the one thing that isn't a node

A node has a type. The editor's palette offers eleven of them. Eight are "record-backed" — the box is a handle to a real Odoo record — plus three structural ones:

Record-backed nodesStructural nodes
ivr (a menu) · queue · call_group · extension · user · voicemail · audio_message · contact start (where the call enters) · outcall (dial out to a number) · hangup (end the call)
⚠️ The classic trap: a Schedule is NOT a node. Business hours ("open / Closed") are an attachment on a start, queue, or call_group node — not a box you drop on the canvas. "Open vs Closed" simply decides which wire the call leaves that node on. Expect this on the quiz: given a list, the odd one out is schedule.(voip_call_flow.py — node attachments)

Flow-owned: the parts belong to the whole

When you drop a new Queue, Call Group or IVR inside a flow, that record is stamped with a callflow_id — it is flow-owned. You cannot edit or delete it from the normal Queues/Groups/Menus lists; it lives and dies with its flow. (The picker that lets you reuse a standalone one filters on callflow_id = false, so you never accidentally grab a flow's private part.)

The symmetry: a flow won't let itself be deleted while something still routes to it — a user's call-forward, another PBX destination. With one sharp exception: a DID pointing at the flow does not block deletion — the number is quietly detached instead. That asymmetry is a favourite interview question.

It compiles — it doesn't run live

Here is the aha that separates people who "get" Call Flows from people who don't. Odoo does not sit in the middle of every call walking your graph. Instead, when the flow changes, voip.pbx.service._get_pbx_destination() acts as a compiler: it walks the graph once and emits the equivalent Wazo/PBX routing config. Asterisk then runs that config at call time, at full speed, with Odoo nowhere in the loop.(pbx_service.py:_get_pbx_destination)

Two consequences fall straight out of "compiler, not interpreter":

  • Cycles. A wire that loops inside one graph is fine (the compiler handles it). But a Call Flow node that points at another Call Flow which points back is a compile-time error — visited_call_flow_ids breaks the recursion so the compiler can't spin forever.
  • Moving boxes is free. Dragging a box to tidy the canvas (its position/size) changes layout, not routing — a signature check skips the PBX sync entirely for layout-only edits. (Validation, though, still runs on every save — a broken graph is caught even if you only nudged a box.)
⌨️ Where this lives in our code

voip/models/voip_call_flow.py — the model, the graph_data JSON, the node-type allow-list, and the ownership rules.
voip/models/pbx_service.py — _get_pbx_destination(), the compiler that turns a graph into PBX config (and recurses only into Call Flows).
voip/static/src/core/flow_editor/ — the visual editor: flow_editor.js, flow_node.js, flow_port.js, plus the geometry/ helpers for drawing nodes and wires; the palette is flow_editor/flow_node_palette.js.

What you actually do in the editor

Drag a node type from the palette onto the canvas; click a node's output port and drag a wire to another node's input; pan and zoom to see the whole map. A menu node shows one output per keypad choice plus a "timeout" wire; a queue or group shows a "no answer" wire. Wire them into the shape you sketched on paper.
